Writing Lab

Show Me the Connection

You made a claim. You included evidence. So why does your professor keep writing “Explain this” or “How does this support your point?”?

Because evidence does not usually explain itself. This lab will help you make the reasoning between your evidence and your point visible to your reader.

Evidence is not the finish line.
Your reader cannot see the connection that exists in your head unless you put that reasoning on the page.

The move

Claim → Evidence → Connection

Step 1CLAIM

What are you trying to show or argue?

Step 2EVIDENCE

What example, quotation, fact, or detail supports that point?

Step 3CONNECTION

How does that evidence support, prove, complicate, or clarify the claim?

See the difference

Evidence With and Without the Connection

Imagine a student is writing about social media and attention.

Without the Connection

Claim: Social media platforms can shape what users pay attention to.

Evidence: A platform’s algorithm may repeatedly place highly engaging posts at the top of a user’s feed.

And then the paragraph moves on.

With the Connection

Claim: Social media platforms can shape what users pay attention to.

Evidence: A platform’s algorithm may repeatedly place highly engaging posts at the top of a user’s feed.

Connection: Because users are more likely to encounter the content that the algorithm repeatedly prioritizes, the platform is not simply showing information neutrally; it is helping determine which topics receive the most attention.

The useful question:
How does this evidence help prove the point I just made?

Connection 1

Which Sentence Actually Explains the Evidence?

Claim: Lack of reliable transportation can make college attendance more difficult for students.

Evidence: A student who depends on two buses may have a commute of more than an hour each way.

Which sentence makes the strongest connection?

Connection 2

Go Beyond Repeating the Evidence

Claim: The character feels increasingly isolated from her family.

Evidence: At dinner, she answers questions with one-word responses and leaves the table before anyone else finishes eating.

Which explanation adds reasoning instead of simply restating the evidence?

Connection 3

What Kind of Connection Do You Need?

Reasoning does more than say, “This proves my claim.” Different evidence may require different kinds of explanation.

Try one of these questions:

  • How does this example show the pattern I am describing?
  • Why does this detail matter?
  • What should the reader notice about this evidence?
  • What does this evidence reveal that is not obvious at first?
  • How does this evidence support my claim?
  • Does this evidence complicate or qualify my point?
  • What conclusion can I reasonably draw from this evidence?
Watch out for the fake connection:
“This quote proves my point because it shows that my claim is true.”

That sounds like explanation, but it does not actually tell the reader how the evidence supports the claim.

The Connection Test

Check the Sentence After Your Evidence

After using evidence, cover up your claim for a moment and look only at the evidence and the sentence after it.

Did I interpret it?Did I explain what the reader should notice about the evidence?
Did I connect it?Did I show how the evidence relates to the point I am making?
Did I add thinking?Did I say something beyond simply repeating the quotation or example?

Your turn

Find the Missing Connection

Open a paragraph from something you are currently writing. Find one place where you use a quotation, fact, example, statistic, or other evidence.

Now revise the actual paragraph.
Spend about 10–15 minutes revising that section so the connection is visible to your reader. You may need one sentence of explanation. You may need several. The goal is not to hit a formula; it is to give the reader the reasoning they need.
