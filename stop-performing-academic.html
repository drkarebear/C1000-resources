Academic Voice Lab

🎭 Stop Performing “Academic”

College writing does not require you to dress every sentence in a tiny suit and hand it a thesaurus.

The goal is not to sound casual. The goal is to sound clear, precise, thoughtful, and appropriate for the situation.

Scene 1 of 8Catch the performance
Scene 1 · The Costume Goes On

“I Need to Sound Smarter”

You know what you want to say:
Real thought: “The policy sounds fair, but it affects working students differently.”
Then you start typing:
School voice: “The multifaceted ramifications of the aforementioned institutional policy illuminate a plethora of inequitable experiential outcomes for employed collegiate individuals.”

What probably happened?

CBT move: catch the rule hiding underneath the sentence: “If I use ordinary language, people will think I’m not smart.” That is a belief to test—not a writing requirement.
Scene 2 · Costume Department

Which Pieces Are Helping?

“The utilization of social media by contemporary adolescents can facilitate meaningful connection, although algorithmic recommendation systems may also intensify exposure to harmful content.”
utilizationfacilitatealgorithmic recommendation systems

Which phrase is most worth keeping because it names something specific?

Not all “academic-sounding” language is bad.
Keep a specialized term when it gives you precision. Question a fancy substitute when a simpler word says the same thing just as accurately.

Scene 4 · Translation Booth

Make It Clear Without Making It Shallow

Original:
“The implementation of the aforementioned policy engenders deleterious consequences for individuals possessing limited economic resources.”

Which revision keeps the idea but removes unnecessary performance?

Clear ≠ simplistic. The strongest revision preserves the claim’s meaning and level of precision while removing words that mainly signal status.
Scene 5 · Don’t Overcorrect

Some Academic Words Earn Their Keep

correlationA specific relationship between variables; useful when that is what you mean.
assumptionNames an idea being taken for granted.
synthesizeNames a particular academic move with multiple sources.
utilizeOften just means use.
aforementionedOften just means this, that, or the actual noun.
plethoraOften just means many—and sometimes adds nothing.

What is the better rule?

Precision test: If I replace this word with a simpler one, do I lose an important distinction? If yes, keep it. If no, the simpler word may be stronger.
Scene 6 · Thought Check

What Are You Afraid the Reader Will Think?

You write: “The article shows that work schedules can affect how much time students have for school.”
Your brain: “That sounds too simple. My professor is going to think I’m not college-level.”

Which response is most evidence-based?

CBT move: change the target from “sound smart” to something you can actually evaluate: Is my idea clear? specific? supportable? developed?
Scene 7 · Upgrade the Thinking, Not the Costume

Make the Idea Smarter

Starting sentence:
“Social media affects students.”

Which revision becomes more academic because the thinking gets more precise?

Academic sophistication often comes from:
specificity · relationships · evidence · qualification · reasoning · acknowledging complexity
