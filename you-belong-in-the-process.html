College Belonging Lab

🌿 You Belong in the Process

Questions, feedback, revision, collaboration, and help-seeking are not signs that you are failing college. They are part of doing college.

This activity is about what successful participation can actually look like when things get confusing, difficult, or unfinished.

Part 1 of 8What belonging looks like
Part 1 · Start Here

Belonging Does Not Mean Never Struggling

Try this idea on:
Successful college students do not succeed because they never get confused, need feedback, ask questions, or revise. They succeed partly because they develop ways to respond when those things happen.

Which statement is closest to what this lab is trying to teach?

Part 2 · Questions

Everyone Else Seems to Get It

Class ends. Your professor has explained the assignment. People start packing up. You still are not sure what the second part of the directions means.

What is a useful college move?

Useful college move: ASK.
Silence does not tell you that everyone else understood. Other students may be unsure too. Questions are one way people learn expectations that are not always obvious.
Part 3 · Collaboration

“I Should Be Able to Do This Myself”

You have reread the same difficult page three times and still are not sure what the author is arguing.

Which responses could be legitimate learning moves?

Collaboration is not the same as giving away your thinking.
Talking through ideas, asking questions, using tutoring, and comparing interpretations can support learning while you still do your own work.
Part 4 · Feedback + Revision

Your Draft Comes Back Covered in Comments

Your professor returns your draft. There are comments in the margins, questions, and suggestions.

Which mental model is more useful?

Feedback is information, not a verdict.
A draft needing revision does not mean the draft “failed.” Revision is part of writing.
Part 5 · The Invisible Classroom

Part 6 · What You See / What You Don't

You Usually See Other People's Finished Moments

WHAT YOU SEE:
A student gives a strong answer in class.
They reread the article twice and asked a friend to talk through one section before class.
WHAT YOU SEE:
A student earns a high grade on an essay.
They went to tutoring, rewrote the thesis, and asked the professor a question about the assignment.
WHAT YOU SEE:
A student seems confident during group work.
They felt lost after the first assignment and spent time figuring out who they could ask for help.
You see other people's visible moments. You live through every messy part of your own process.
Part 7 · Help-Seeking

You Do Not Have to Begin With “I Don't Understand Anything”

1. Check the directions again.
2. Identify the exact place you're unsure.
3. Ask a classmate or use course support.
4. Use tutoring / Writing Center / embedded support.
5. Ask the professor a specific question.

Which question gives the professor the most useful information?

Specific questions make help easier to give.
Instead of asking someone to do the thinking for you, ask for information that helps you make the next decision.
