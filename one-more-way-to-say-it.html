🎛️ One More Way to Say It

What if teaching academic writing could give students more ways to communicate rather than replacing the language resources they already have?

No score. No LJE purity test. About 7 minutes.

View 1 of 9The tension
Start here

Do We Have to Choose?

A familiar tension in college writing instruction sounds something like this:

Concern A

Students need access to academic conventions.

We don't want to withhold knowledge that may matter in college, disciplines, workplaces, and other rhetorical situations.

Concern B

Students' existing language isn't defective.

We don't want “academic” to quietly become a synonym for smarter, better, more legitimate, or more worthy.

🦝 Raccoon has located a false binary.

“What if the student gets to keep both?”

Idea to test: Academic conventions can become one more rhetorical repertoire—another set of choices a writer can understand and use intentionally.
Scene 1 · The framing

How Would You Introduce “Academic Writing”?

First week of class

You're introducing expectations for an ENGL C1000 argument. Students will need to make claims, use evidence, explain reasoning, and make language choices appropriate to the rhetorical situation.

Which explanation would you be most likely to give?

Turn the idea around

The middle framing offers access without presenting assimilation as the price of access. Students still learn the conventions. But the reason isn't “this is how good writers sound.” The conventions become tools connected to readers, purposes, genres, disciplines, and contexts.

🦝 Raccoon Check

“Teach the tool. Maybe don't tell them it's the only tool in the shed.”

Scene 2 · Access

But What If the Convention Really Matters?

Teaching moment

A student has never used signal phrases to distinguish their own ideas from source material. In this assignment, readers need to be able to tell who is saying what.

What does an LJE lens ask you to do?

Access matters

Linguistic justice doesn't require less instruction. Here, explicit teaching can increase the student's options. The useful shift is explaining what the convention helps a writer and reader accomplish rather than presenting it as evidence of “proper” English.

🦝 Raccoon Check

“More agency usually requires more information, not less.”

Scene 3 · Repertoire

Same Writer. Another Situation.

Student language

A student says to a friend: “They seriously need more night classes. How are you supposed to work all day and still take the classes you need?”

Now the student wants to make the same argument in an evidence-based college essay.

What are we helping the writer learn?

Rhetorical flexibility

The text to a friend and the academic argument do different work. The academic version may change—but that doesn't retroactively make the first version “bad English.” A flexible writer gains another way to communicate the idea.

🦝 Raccoon Check

“Different room. Different choices. Same writer.”

Scene 4 · What and when

“Never Start a Sentence With ‘But.’”

Convention alert

You encounter a student sentence beginning with But. Your first impulse is to mark it.

What question creates more rhetorical knowledge?

From right/wrong to what/when

Starting a sentence with And or But can create emphasis or signal a relationship. The more useful teaching question may be whether that is the effect the writer wants—not whether a remembered school rule has been violated.

🦝 Raccoon Check

“Before we confiscate the word, what crime did it commit?”

Scene 5 · Critical transparency

A Convention With Consequences

Teaching moment

A convention is strongly expected in a particular academic genre. Ignoring it could have real consequences for a student's grade or how readers perceive the work.

Which response gives the student the most useful knowledge?

Teach it—and make it visible

Critical transparency doesn't pretend conventions are neutral, and it doesn't hide them from students. It makes expectations available for use and examination: purpose, context, variation, history, alternatives, benefits, and risks.

🦝 Raccoon Check

“Knowing the rules and believing the rules are natural laws are different things.”

Scene 6 · Voice

What Might the Revision Lose?

Feedback

You can make a student's sentence more conventionally formal. The revision is grammatically clean and sounds recognizably “academic”—but it also removes some urgency, rhythm, and personality.

What should happen next?

Choice requires tradeoffs

LJE-informed instruction doesn't require one predetermined answer. It can help writers notice consequences and make increasingly informed choices about clarity, credibility, voice, identity, genre, and reader expectations.

🦝 Raccoon Check

“Could we get the clarity without evicting the writer?”

Your turn

Try the Reframe

A familiar direction

“Use more academic language.”

Rewrite that direction so the student still gets useful guidance, but the reason for the language choice becomes visible. You might name the reader, purpose, genre, desired effect, clarity issue, or convention.

You don't need a perfect script. The shift is from “sound like an academic writer” toward helping the student understand what a particular language choice could accomplish.

One More Way to Say It

The goal isn't to give students fewer expectations. And it isn't to pretend rhetorical situations have no conventions or consequences.

ACCESS
Teach conventions students may need.
TRANSPARENCY
Explain what those conventions do, where they tend to be expected, and how they vary.
REPERTOIRE
Frame new conventions as additions to—not proof of the inadequacy of—students' existing language resources.
AGENCY
Give writers enough knowledge and practice to make increasingly intentional choices.
🦝 One last question

“Are we giving the writer one more way to say it—or telling them there's only one?”
