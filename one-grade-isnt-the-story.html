Grade Reset Tool

One Grade Isn’t the Story

You saw the grade. Maybe your stomach dropped. Maybe you got angry, embarrassed, discouraged, or immediately started predicting what it means.

You do not have to pretend the grade does not matter. But before you decide what it says about you, your writing, or the rest of the course, let’s find out what the grade can actually tell you.

A grade is information about a performance.

Your brain may turn that information into a much bigger story. This tool will help you separate the two and decide what to do next.

Step 1

What Did Your Brain Say?

You do not have to enter your grade. Start with the thought that showed up when you saw it.

Step 2

Separate the Grade From the Story

Compare these:

WHAT I KNOW I earned 67% on this assignment.
WHAT MY BRAIN ADDED I am bad at college.
WHAT I KNOW My professor wrote several comments on my draft.
WHAT MY BRAIN ADDED My professor thinks I am a bad writer.
The first statement is information.

The second is an interpretation, prediction, or judgment. It may feel true without being something the grade actually proves.

Step 3

Look at the Evidence Before You Decide What It Means

If feedback or a rubric is available, open it now.

Do not try to fix the paper yet. Your job is only to answer:

Where did the points actually go?

Choose the area that seems to have affected the grade most. You are identifying a problem to work on—not describing yourself.

Step 4

Turn a Judgment Into a Problem You Can Work On

Compare:

IDENTITY JUDGMENT I’m bad at writing.
SPECIFIC PROBLEM I need to explain how my evidence supports my claim.

The second statement gives you somewhere to go.

Step 5

What Is Changeable?

You cannot change the work you already submitted. You may be able to change what happens next.

Step 6

If You Don’t Understand the Grade, Don’t Guess

Sometimes you look at the rubric and still do not understand what happened. That does not mean you need a better attitude. It means you need more information.

Turn confusion into a question.

Instead of: “Why did you give me this grade?”

Try identifying the part you want to understand:

“I saw that I lost points in analysis. Could you help me understand one place where I could have explained my evidence more fully?”

Step 7

Build a More Accurate Story

This is not about replacing a negative thought with a positive one. Make the thought more specific, accurate, and useful.

TOO BIG I’m terrible at writing.
MORE ACCURATE I had trouble developing the analysis in this essay. That is something I can practice.
Step 8

Make One Move

You do not need to fix the entire grade today. Choose one useful action and actually begin it.

My Grade Reset Card

What my brain said This grade means something bigger about me.
What I actually know I received information about one assignment.
The specific issue I found I need to understand the feedback more clearly.
A more accurate story One assignment can show me something to work on without defining me as a writer.
What I need to ask I do not need additional information right now.
My next move Use what I learned to make one useful change.
Sometimes the reaction is bigger than the assignment.

If you are too overwhelmed to look at the feedback, unsure what your academic options are, or having a hard time getting back into the course, another person can be part of the next move. A professor, tutor, counselor, or appropriate college support person can help you figure out what comes next.

One grade is information. It is not your whole story.

You know more now than you did before you looked at the feedback.

Use that information for your next move.
