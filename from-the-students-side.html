Faculty Learning Lab · ENGL C1000

👀 From the Student's Side

You know your course because you built it. Students encounter it from the other direction. In this lab, you'll look at familiar teaching choices, make a judgment from the instructor side, and then turn the camera around.

No score. No “gotcha.” About 7 minutes.

Same Course. Different View.

Most teaching choices make sense from the instructor side. That's not the question we're testing.

The question: What does this choice require a student to notice, know, infer, navigate, or decide?
🦝 Raccoon Check

“You know the course because you built the course.”

For each scene, choose the response closest to your first reaction. Then reveal the student side. Sometimes the original teaching choice will work just fine.

Scene 1 · Independence

“Students Can Ask If They're Confused.”

Instructor side

Your assignment sheet includes the prompt, requirements, and due date. You don't want to over-explain. College students need opportunities to become independent, and you tell them to ask questions if anything is unclear.

What is your first reaction?

Student side

“I read the directions twice. I think I understand them. I don't know that I'm misunderstanding the word analyze, so I don't have a question to ask.”

🦝 Raccoon Check

“You can't ask about the thing you don't know you don't know.”

Possible move: Independence can include a checkpoint: a sample response, a “before you begin” question, a short task-decoding activity, or an invitation to paraphrase the assignment. The student still does the thinking.
Scene 2 · Feedback

“I Gave Them Detailed Feedback.”

Instructor side

You spend substantial time responding to a draft. You mark unclear sentences, MLA problems, weak transitions, missing analysis, paragraph structure, grammar patterns, source integration, and places where the thesis needs work.

What seems most important?

Student side

“There are comments everywhere. I fixed the commas because I knew how. I wasn't sure which of the other 41 things mattered most.”

🦝 Raccoon Check

“Forty-one priorities is a fascinating number of priorities.”

Possible move: Signal the one to three changes with the greatest payoff. Detailed feedback isn't inherently bad; the student needs help seeing a path through it.
Scene 3 · Course Navigation

“It's All in Canvas.”

Instructor side

The assignment instructions are on the assignment page. The rubric is attached there. The model is in Files. You announced a deadline adjustment last week. Feedback appears in Grades. Everything students need is available.

What would you check first?

Student side

“I found the assignment. I didn't know there was a model in Files. I saw the announcement about the new deadline, but now I can't remember where it was. I think my professor left comments, but I'm not sure where to open them.”

🦝 Raccoon Check

“Available and findable are cousins. Not twins.”

Possible move: Give each major task a clear home base that points outward to the rubric, model, feedback location, and current deadline.
Scene 4 · Modeling

“Am I Doing Too Much for Them?”

Instructor side

Before students analyze their own passage, you project a different paragraph. You underline a detail, think aloud about two possible interpretations, reject the weaker one, and explain why the stronger interpretation better fits the text.

What does this do to the rigor?

Student side

“Oh. Analysis isn't just finding a quote and explaining what happened. I saw how she moved from the detail to an interpretation—and that she considered an idea that didn't work.”

🦝 Raccoon Check

“Support happened. Thinking is still required. Everybody survives.”

What worked: The instructor exposed the process without completing the students' task. Not every student-side flip reveals a problem.
Scene 5 · Standards

“The Rubric Is Accurate.”

Instructor side

A student's paper earns a low score in analysis. Your rubric accurately identifies the problem, and your end comment says: “Needs more analysis of evidence.”

What might still be missing?

Student side

“I understand that my analysis is weak. I still don't know what I was supposed to write after the quote.”

🦝 Raccoon Check

“A diagnosis can be completely accurate and still not be directions.”

Possible move: Keep the standard. Add a next-step cue: “After this quotation, tell me what specific words/details you want the reader to notice and how they support your claim.”
Scene 6 · Help-Seeking

“I Told Them I'm Available.”

Instructor side

Your syllabus lists office hours, and you regularly tell the class: “Come see me if you need help. That's what office hours are for.”

What else might affect whether students come?

Student side

“I know she has office hours. I don't know if my problem is important enough. Also, I don't really know what to ask. I mostly just know I'm lost.”

🦝 Raccoon Check

“Availability and approachability aren't quite the same thing.”

Possible move: Normalize specific reasons to come: “You can bring a paragraph, a confusing comment, the assignment directions, or just the sentence ‘I don't know where I'm stuck.’”

Same Classroom. Different View.

Most teaching choices make sense from the instructor side. The useful question is what those choices require students to notice, know, infer, navigate, or decide from their side.

🚧 Directions
Can students recognize what they don't yet understand?
💬 Feedback
Can students tell what matters most and what to do next?
🧭 Navigation
Is important information merely available—or findable from where students begin?
🧠 Modeling
Can we expose expert thinking while leaving the intellectual work to students?
🚪 Help
Does “I'm available” also tell students how to enter?
🦝 One last rummage

“What does this look like if I don't already know what I know?”

Try This Tomorrow: Pick one routine part of your course—an announcement, assignment, feedback comment, Canvas module, late-work policy, or invitation to office hours. Look at it from the starting point of a student encountering it for the first time.
