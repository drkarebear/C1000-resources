For ENGL C1000 instructors

Make the invisible parts of college writing easier to teach.

Use short faculty learning labs to examine common teaching moments, then connect those ideas to student-facing mini-labs you can assign, embed, or use in class.

This is not a checklist of “best practices.” It is a place to test teaching choices, make expert processes more visible, and preserve rigor while reducing unnecessary friction.

Start with a teaching problem.

Choose the moment you want to improve: reading, assignment design, feedback, persistence, or something else.

Try one small change.

Faculty labs are designed around practical decisions you can test rather than broad advice you are expected to “implement.”

Connect it to student practice.

Each pathway points to student mini-labs that make the same reading, writing, or help-seeking move visible to learners.

Make Reading More Teachable

Move beyond “do the reading” by making expert reading decisions visible. ⌄
Teaching problem: Students may complete a reading without knowing what to prioritize, how to repair confusion, or how the reading is supposed to become material for writing.

Faculty lab · coming nextThe Hidden Reading CurriculumIdentify which reading moves your assignments assume students already know—and which ones might need to be taught explicitly.
Faculty lab · coming nextWhen “They Didn’t Do the Reading” Isn’t the Whole StoryDistinguish non-completion from comprehension breakdown, purpose confusion, overload, and weak reading-to-writing transfer.

Student tools you can use now

Student mini-labWait, What Did I Just Read?Students practice repairing comprehension when a sentence or paragraph stops making sense.Useful when: students reread passively, get stuck on dense prose, or interpret confusion as failure. Student mini-labRead Like You Have a PurposeStudents practice reading and annotating for a specific task instead of trying to mark everything.Useful when: annotation is indiscriminate or students cannot tell what matters for the assignment. Student mini-labActive ReadingStudents practice interacting with the text rather than moving through pages without monitoring meaning.Useful when: students report “I read it, but nothing stuck.” Student mini-labDecode the AssignmentStudents separate the task, requirements, choices, and evidence of success in a prompt.Useful when: assignment language itself is creating reading load or task confusion.

Make Writing Processes Visible

Teach the moves inside the product—not only the qualities of a finished essay. ⌄
Teaching problem: Instructions such as “develop this,” “analyze more,” or “write a strong thesis” describe outcomes but may not reveal the decisions an experienced writer makes to produce them.

Faculty mini-labs

Faculty lab · coming nextWhat Does an Expert Writer Do That Students Can’t See?Surface the planning, testing, rereading, restructuring, and decision-making hidden inside fluent academic writing.
Faculty lab · coming nextTeach the Move, Not Just the ProductTurn broad criteria such as “analysis,” “development,” and “organization” into actions students can practice.
Faculty lab · coming nextWhy “Write a Strong Thesis” Isn’t an InstructionExamine what students need to notice and decide before they can create a useful working claim.
Faculty lab · coming nextFrom “Needs Development” to a Teachable Next StepTranslate a common writing weakness into a specific reasoning move students can attempt.

Student tools you can use now

Student mini-labThe Analysis DetectiveMoves students from clue → pattern → interpretation → significance.Useful when: students summarize accurately but struggle to generate analysis. Student mini-labMake Your CaseHelps students turn an idea into an arguable, supportable claim.Useful when: claims are descriptive, obvious, or too broad to develop. Student mini-labShow Me the ConnectionMakes claim → evidence → reasoning visible.Useful when: students include evidence but leave the warrant implicit. Student mini-labDevelop, Don’t PadTeaches expansion through explanation, specificity, and reasoning rather than word-count inflation.Useful when: students equate development with adding sentences. Student mini-labReverse OutlineStudents diagnose what each paragraph is currently doing after a draft exists.Useful when: organization advice feels abstract or students revise sentence-by-sentence only. Student mini-labThe Writing ProcessBreaks writing into different jobs rather than presenting the essay as one giant act.Useful when: students expect drafting, revising, and editing to happen simultaneously.

Design for the Brain Students Actually Bring to Class

Preserve complexity while reducing avoidable cognitive and procedural friction. ⌄
Teaching problem: A task can be appropriately rigorous and still contain unnecessary barriers—unclear first steps, hidden expectations, overloaded directions, or too many decisions at once.

Faculty mini-labs

Faculty lab · coming nextIs This Assignment Hard—or Hard to Decode?Separate disciplinary challenge from ambiguity, navigation burden, and hidden procedural knowledge.
Faculty lab · coming nextHow Much Are Students Holding in Working Memory?Notice when instructions, new concepts, source material, and writing demands compete for the same limited attention.
Faculty lab · coming nextMake the First Step SmallerDesign an entry point that gets students into meaningful work without simplifying the final intellectual task.
Faculty lab · coming nextProductive Struggle or Unnecessary Friction?Decide which difficulty belongs to learning and which difficulty is merely consuming students’ energy.

Student tools you can use now

Student mini-labFocus RescueStudents identify the kind of stuck they are experiencing and test a matching strategy.Useful when: “just focus” or generic time-management advice is not enough. Student mini-labStudy LabStudents notice, predict, test, and adjust their study conditions rather than relying on fixed “learning styles.”Useful when: students need metacognitive practice with task-condition matching. Student mini-labGood Enough to DraftSeparates discovery from polishing so students can begin before they know the final wording.Useful when: perfectionism makes drafting unusually effortful. Student mini-labCrack the Academic CodeStudents translate academic task language into the kind of thinking or writing it requires.Useful when: familiar-looking academic verbs still hide expectations.

Support Belonging Without Lowering the Rigor

Help difficulty remain a learning problem instead of becoming evidence that a student does not belong. ⌄
Teaching problem: Students can interpret confusion, revision, comparison, or help-seeking as signs that they are not “college material,” even when those experiences are normal parts of advanced learning.

Faculty mini-labs

Faculty lab · coming nextBelonging Is Not the Same as ReassuranceCompare responses to student struggle and identify which ones preserve standards, agency, and a credible path forward.
Faculty lab · coming nextWhat Does the Student Hear?Examine how ordinary classroom language can be interpreted when a student already doubts whether they belong.
Faculty lab · coming nextNormalize the Process, Not the OutcomeMake struggle, revision, questions, and uncertainty visible without promising that every attempt will succeed.
Faculty lab · coming nextRigor + a Way InTest whether expectations remain intellectually demanding while still giving students a usable entry point.

Student tools you can use now

Student mini-labStill a WriterStudents separate a difficult writing event from a global judgment about their ability.Useful when: a grade or comment has become “I’m just bad at writing.” Student mini-labYou Belong in the ProcessMakes questions, feedback, revision, collaboration, and help-seeking visible as normal college participation.Useful when: students mistake needing support for not belonging. Student mini-labYou’re Seeing Their Highlight ReelHelps students distinguish what they can observe about peers from the conclusions they draw about themselves.Useful when: social comparison is undermining participation or persistence. Student mini-labWhat If I Ask a Dumb Question?Students separate evidence from prediction and turn uncertainty into a specific question.Useful when: fear of appearing unprepared prevents clarification or help-seeking.

Give Feedback Students Can Actually Use

Treat comments as part of instruction, not merely a record of what was wrong. ⌄
Teaching problem: Feedback can be accurate but still unusable if students cannot translate it into priorities, revision decisions, or concrete next actions.

Faculty mini-labs

Faculty lab · coming nextWhat Does the Student Hear When You Write “Unclear”?Test common margin comments for how much actionable information they actually provide.
Faculty lab · coming nextComment Less, Teach MorePrioritize comments that can change the draft rather than documenting every problem in it.
Faculty lab · coming nextWhich Comment Comes First?Practice sequencing feedback so high-level changes are not buried beneath sentence-level correction.
Faculty lab · coming nextFeedback or Autopsy?Distinguish comments that help a learner make a future decision from comments that only explain the finished paper’s weaknesses.

Student tools you can use now

Student mini-labProfessor Comment DecoderStudents translate broad comments into possible revision actions.Useful when: feedback language is familiar to instructors but opaque to students. Student mini-labThe Editing RoomStudents classify and prioritize feedback before revising.Useful when: students fix easy surface issues first or try to respond to every comment equally. Student mini-labReverse OutlineGives students a way to inspect organization using the draft that actually exists.Useful when: feedback says “organization” but the student needs a diagnostic method. Student mini-labProofreading ERMoves sentence-level correction later in the process and prioritizes high-impact issues.Useful when: students equate revision with proofreading.

Keep Students Connected

Design clearer ways to ask for help, recover after disruption, and return to the work. ⌄
Teaching problem: “Reach out if you need help” sounds simple from the instructor side, but a student may still need to identify the problem, decide whom to contact, overcome embarrassment, formulate a question, and understand whether returning is still possible.

Faculty mini-labs

Faculty lab · coming nextThe Student Who DisappearsExamine the decisions and emotions between one missed task and full disengagement—and where course design can create a return path.
Faculty lab · coming nextWhat Does “Reach Out If You Need Help” Require?Make the hidden help-seeking workload visible and test ways to reduce ambiguity without removing student agency.
Faculty lab · coming nextDesigning a Way Back InConsider how students can re-enter the course after missed work without pretending the disruption did not matter.
Faculty lab · coming nextWhen Flexibility Helps—and When Ambiguity HurtsDistinguish compassionate options from unclear expectations that create even more decision load.

Student tools you can use now

Student mini-labDon’t DisappearHelps students reconnect after missed work or avoidance.Useful when: embarrassment or overwhelm is turning a small disruption into disengagement. Student mini-labWhat’s Still Possible?Students identify actions that can still affect the course rather than treating past setbacks as irreversible.Useful when: all-or-nothing thinking follows missed work or a low grade. Student mini-labOne Grade Isn’t the StoryHelps students interpret one score as information rather than as a complete forecast.Useful when: a single grade is driving withdrawal, avoidance, or hopelessness. Student mini-labWho Do I Ask?Students choose the appropriate human or campus resource for the kind of problem they have.Useful when: students know they need help but do not know where to start. Student mini-labWhat If I Ask a Dumb Question?Students practice asking for clarification without outsourcing the thinking.Useful when: fear and uncertainty block office hours, tutoring, email, or class questions. Student mini-labSubmit the Imperfect ThingHelps students distinguish a meaningful missing requirement from endless polishing.Useful when: perfectionism is about to turn assessable work into a non-submission.
Design principle: Support does not have to mean removing consequences or lowering expectations. Often the most useful intervention is making the next decision clearer and the return path visible.

A simple lens for using the collection

When a student is struggling, ask what part of the process is currently invisible. The mini-labs are designed to make one of those hidden moves easier to see and practice.

What is the learner trying to do?Name the real intellectual task, not only the assignment product.
What decision is hidden?Identify the expert move a student may not yet know they are supposed to make.
What can they practice now?Give the learner a small authentic move that transfers back into the course work.
