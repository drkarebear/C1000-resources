🪜 Scaffold It, Don’t Simplify It

Can we make the path clearer without making the thinking easier? In this short teaching simulation, you’ll protect the intellectual work of a college-level task while deciding what support helps students actually enter it.

No score. No perfect-teacher test. About 6–8 minutes.

What Are We Trying to Protect?

A student struggles with a demanding task. Two reactions can feel tempting:

Reaction 1

“They’re not ready for this.”

Reduce the reading, simplify the task, lower the complexity.

Reaction 2

“College is supposed to be hard.”

Keep every obstacle and assume struggle itself proves rigor.

The third move: identify the intellectual work that actually matters, keep it, and make the path to that work more visible and doable.
🦝 Raccoon’s working theory

“Tiny chunks ≠ tiny ideas.”

A Difficult Scholarly Reading

The task

Students need to read a challenging article and identify the author’s central claim, reasoning, and use of evidence before discussing it.

What changed?

The middle option changes the entry conditions, not the intellectual target. Students still interpret the original article. The scaffold makes expert reading moves visible, limits unnecessary working-memory demands, and gives students a repeatable process.

Round 2 · Analysis

“They Keep Summarizing Instead of Analyzing.”

The task

Students can identify evidence, but many paragraphs stop after a quotation or paraphrase. You want them to explain how the evidence supports the claim and why it matters.

What would you add?

The hidden move

“Analyze more” names the desired outcome, but it doesn’t necessarily teach the move. A worked example plus a think-aloud exposes the thinking that experienced writers may perform automatically. The assignment remains analytical; students get a bridge into the reasoning.

🦝 Raccoon notices something invisible.

“If we can do it automatically, students may not know there was a step there.”

Round 3 · Research

A Big Research Assignment

The task

Students must develop a research question, find credible sources, synthesize them, and make an evidence-based argument. Several students freeze after reading the prompt.

Which redesign is most likely to support rigor rather than dilute it?

Sequence ≠ simplification

Staging complex work can reduce planning overload while preserving the authentic decisions students need to make. The eventual task remains research, synthesis, argument, and revision. The scaffold temporarily organizes the process so students can practice those moves rather than spending all their energy figuring out where to begin.

🦝 Raccoon has a clipboard now.

“We can sequence the mountain without turning it into a speed bump.”

Round 4 · Revision

A Student Says, “But I Already Fixed It.”

The task

You ask for substantial revision. The student corrects commas, changes a few words, and resubmits. They genuinely believe they revised.

Which response teaches the more complex version of revision?

A scaffold can change the student's mental model

Here the difficulty is partly conceptual: the student may not yet know what substantive revision looks like. Contrasting examples make the distinction concrete while preserving the expectation that writers rethink ideas, evidence, organization, and reasoning.

🦝 Raccoon translates.

“Don’t just say ‘go deeper.’ Show me what deeper does.”

Round 5 · Online learning

The Assignment Is Rigorous. The Navigation Is Too.

The situation

In an online section, students must open four pages, locate a PDF in a module, remember a discussion deadline listed elsewhere, and return to a separate assignment page to submit the work.

What counts as a scaffold here?

Not every difficulty is academic rigor

Students do need independence, but remembering where information lives is not the same intellectual work as reading, reasoning, writing, or research. Predictable navigation can free attention for the course's actual learning goals.

🦝 Important distinction

“Canvas hide-and-seek is not one of the course outcomes.”

Round 6 · When support works

Should the Scaffold Stay Forever?

Later in the semester

Early in the term, you gave students a paragraph frame that prompted: claim → evidence → reasoning → significance. Students have now practiced the pattern several times and many use it independently.

What is the next teaching move?

Scaffolds have an exit strategy

A scaffold is most powerful when it helps learners perform increasingly independently. If a support hardens into a permanent formula, it can eventually constrain the thinking it was meant to enable. Fade structure as students gain control, while keeping support available when useful.

🦝 Raccoon lets go of the ladder.

“The goal was never to carry the ladder forever.”

The pattern

Before You Add a Scaffold, Ask Four Questions

1. What is the real intellectual work?Interpret? Analyze? Synthesize? Make an argument? Revise? Make a rhetorical decision?
2. What is making entry unnecessarily hard?Hidden steps? Working-memory load? Unfamiliar genre? Navigation? Missing model? Too many simultaneous decisions?
3. What support preserves the thinking?Think-aloud, worked example, chunking, sequence, checklist, prompt, guided practice, model, predictable course pathway.
4. How will the support fade?Move from model → guided attempt → independent choice. Keep optional tools available without making the scaffold the destination.
A useful test: After adding the support, ask, “Who is doing the important thinking now?” If the instructor, template, or shortcut is doing the core intellectual work for the student, the support may have crossed into simplification.

Why this approach is brain-friendly

This lab models the same design principle it teaches: one decision at a time, brief scenarios, repeated structure, immediate explanation, visible progress, and a final synthesis after the examples. The goal is to reduce extraneous cognitive demands without reducing conceptual complexity.
